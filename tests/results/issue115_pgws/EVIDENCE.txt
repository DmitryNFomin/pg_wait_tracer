#115 FOLLOW-UP — does pg_wait_sampling carry the scheduler-induced sampling
bias we measured in our own sampled tier?

Short answer: YES, the mechanism is confirmed and directly observed, but it is
smaller than ours — and pg_wait_sampling's CPU number is wrong for a second,
larger, unrelated reason that has nothing to do with scheduling.

Machine    Hetzner cx33 (4 vCPU), pgwt=gate-snapshot image, server id

           kernel.sched_schedstats=1, PG 18.6 port 5418, pgbench scale 10

================================================================================
0. HOW pg_wait_sampling ACTUALLY SAMPLES  (read before concluding)
================================================================================

collector.c: one background worker loops on
WaitLatch(&MyProc->procLatch, ..., time_to_sleep, PG_WAIT_EXTENSION), and on
each wakeup, if the profile period has elapsed, calls probe_waits(), which
takes ProcArrayLock LW_SHARED and walks ProcGlobal->allProcs[] reading each
PGPROC's wait_event_info. It is therefore exactly the shape the claim
describes: a task that must be scheduled before it can observe.

Three things about it are genuinely different from polling pg_stat_activity,
and all three are to its credit:

 a. It reads ONE field, PGPROC->wait_event_info, straight out of shared
    memory. pg_stat_activity's `state` and `wait_event` come from different
    structures, which is what makes a row torn; pg_wait_sampling has nothing
    to tear. Measured torn rows in the pg_stat_activity reference over the
    same windows: 0.71 % saturated, 2.31 % unsaturated. For pg_wait_sampling
    the corresponding number is structurally zero.
 b. Its period is self-correcting: time_to_sleep is computed from elapsed
    time, not from a fixed sleep after variable work. Measured effective
    probe rate: 100.00 Hz in every one of the six runs, saturated and not,
    against the pg_stat_activity poller's nominal 50 Hz that actually
    delivered 48.6 Hz. The "we deliberately skip some samplings" path in
    collector.c never fired at this load.
 c. Its probe is cheap: one uint32 read per backend. Measured on-CPU burst
    per probe, 8 backends: mean 42 us, p50 in [32,64) us, saturated and
    unsaturated alike. It is almost never preempted mid-walk, so the eight
    per-backend reads that share one timestamp really are near-simultaneous.
    (With hundreds of backends the walk would be longer and this would stop
    being true.)

And one thing that is a real, structural limitation:

 d. It has no `state`. With sample_cpu = on (the default) every backend whose
    wait_event_info is 0 is counted as CPU, whether it is executing a
    statement or sitting between pgstat_report_activity(idle) and the next
    ClientRead doing protocol work. Its CPU share is therefore a
    WHOLE-BACKEND number, structurally the same quantity pgsa_variants.py
    calls V2 — not the in-command number our tiers and the conventional
    pg_stat_activity classifier (V1) report. Comparing it against V1 or
    against our tiers' own share would be comparing two different
    definitions; every table below puts it against the whole-backend
    versions of the exact tier and of the kernel.

================================================================================
2. THE MECHANISM, MEASURED DIRECTLY  (not inferred from the symptom)
================================================================================

The collector background worker was identified as a child of the postmaster
(`pg_wait_sampling collector`) and put through exactly the instrument used on
our own samplers: sched:sched_wakeup and sched:sched_switch, comparing the
fraction of backends on-CPU-or-runnable measured BY TIME against the same
fraction measured AT THE INSTANTS the observer was switched in.

"pgws_probe" counts only the switch-ins that follow a wakeup — the top of the
collector's loop, which is where probe_waits() runs. Self-check that this is
the right set of instants: wakeup-triggered switch-ins were 109.5/s saturated
and 102.1/s unsaturated, against the 100.0 Hz effective probe rate the profile
itself reports (the surplus is this harness's own 2 Hz profile reads, each of
which wakes the collector out of band, plus a few latch wakeups).

  quantity                                saturated (8)      unsaturated (2)
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  backends on-CPU-or-runnable, by time    73.00 [72.67..73.36]  67.35 [67.33..67.36]
  ... at the pgws collector's probes      70.33 [70.13..70.70]  65.33 [64.21..66.55]
  ... at the pg_stat_activity poller's    68.73 [68.07..69.20]  66.03 [65.15..67.05]

  BIAS, pp of WALL on-or-runnable
    pg_wait_sampling collector              -2.67 [-2.81..-2.53]  -2.02 [-3.12..-0.79]
    pg_stat_activity poller                 -4.27 [-4.60..-3.77]  -1.32 [-2.18..-0.30]
  THE SAME BIAS, pp of the CPU SHARE
    pg_wait_sampling collector              -2.96 [-3.11..-2.81]  -2.55 [-3.93..-0.99]
    pg_stat_activity poller                 -6.13 [-6.57..-5.44]  -1.61 [-2.63..-0.36]

(pp of wall is converted to pp of the share by dividing by that observer's own
non-idle fraction of wall — 90.1 % for pg_wait_sampling, 69.6 % for the
pg_stat_activity poller saturated. They are different denominators because
pg_wait_sampling keeps the out-of-command CPU rows its classifier cannot
exclude. Confusing these two units is what made #115's earlier "6.4 vs 3.7 pp"
look like a disagreement.)

The visible cause, the same one we found for our own sampler — the observer's
own wakeup-to-running delay:

  observer wakeup -> running delay        saturated          unsaturated
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  pg_wait_sampling collector   mean        145 us              16 us
                               p50 bucket  [16,32) us          [8,16) us
                               p90 bucket  [256,512) us        [16,32) us
  pg_stat_activity poller      mean        303 us              15 us
                               p50 bucket  [128,256) us        [8,16) us
                               p90 bucket  [512,1K) us         [8,16) us

Saturated, the collector waits for a processor 0.65 s over the 39 s window
against the 0.21 s it spends running — it spends three times longer wanting a
processor than using one, which IS the mechanism, made visible.

WHY ITS BIAS IS SMALLER THAN THE POLLER'S. The displacement of an observer's
sample instants is set by how long it has to wait for a processor, and that
wait is set by how much work it does per sample. The collector does one uint32
read per backend (42 us per probe) and gets on a processor in 16-32 us at the
median; the pg_stat_activity poller clears its snapshot, walks every backend's
status entry and inserts eight rows (about twice the CPU per sample) and waits
128-256 us at the median. Roughly half the delay, roughly half the bias:
-2.67 pp against -4.27 pp of wall. The bias is not a property of "being a
sampler" in the abstract — it scales with the sampler's own service demand.
That also predicts pg_wait_sampling's advantage shrinks as the backend count
grows and the ProcArray walk gets longer, which this run (8 backends) cannot
show.

================================================================================
3. WHAT IS NOT SCHEDULER BIAS
================================================================================

The -8.19 pp gap to the kernel saturated is NOT all sampling bias. Decomposed:

  pg_wait_sampling - kernel                          -8.19 pp
    of which measured scheduler bias (share units)   -2.96 pp
    of which our exact tier is also below the kernel -5.72 pp

That -5.72 pp is the exact tier's own distance from the kernel over the same
window and is a definitional matter recorded in #115's reconciliation (the
kernel counts run-queue delay of processes that PostgreSQL is not calling
"in a wait", and the tiers discard out-of-command CPU differently); it is not
pg_wait_sampling's fault. Against the exact tier, which is the closest
instrument #115 has to ground truth, pg_wait_sampling is -2.48 pp saturated —
consistent, within the spread, with the -2.96 pp of directly measured
scheduling bias alone. The scheduling bias accounts for essentially the whole
of pg_wait_sampling's error against the exact tier.

================================================================================
4. ITS OWN OVERHEAD  (it perturbs the system it measures, but very little)
================================================================================

  quantity (39.00 s window, 8 backends)     saturated       unsaturated
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  collector on-CPU, schedstat f1            0.21 s          0.21 s
    as a share of ONE processor             0.54 %          0.54 %
    as a share of this 4-vCPU box           0.13 %          0.13 %
  collector run-queue delay, schedstat f2   0.65 s          0.03 s
  on-CPU per probe (sched_switch)           42 us mean      41 us mean
  LWLock:ProcArray seen by the backends     0.06 % of       0.03 % of
    (upper bound: not all of it is pgws)      non-idle        non-idle

For scale, over the same windows: the pg_stat_activity poller used 1.02 % of
one processor (twice as much), and our own pg_wait_tracer daemon in escalated
tiered mode used 1.75 s = 4.5 % of one processor, eight times the collector.
pg_wait_sampling is the cheapest of the three observers in this comparison.

Two honest caveats on the figures above. (1) The collector's on-CPU time
includes serving this harness's 2 Hz reads of pg_wait_sampling_profile, each
of which serialises the whole profile hash through a shm_mq; those are 2 of
its ~102 activations per second but much longer than an ordinary probe (the
p90 burst tail), so 0.54 % is an UPPER bound on stock overhead. (2) A
throughput A/B with the extension off was not attempted: run-to-run tps spread
here is about 8 % (2519..2723), an order of magnitude larger than a 0.13 %
CPU cost could move, so such a test could not have resolved it either way. The
direct kernel-side measurement is the stronger evidence and is what is quoted.

================================================================================
5. CONTROLS: does the sampling PERIOD explain any of it?
================================================================================

Two objections had to be ruled out by measurement rather than by argument:
(a) "its bias is smaller only because it samples at 100 Hz against the
poller's 49 Hz", and (b) "at exactly 100.00 Hz a strictly periodic sampler
could be aliasing against a quasi-periodic workload". Only
pg_wait_sampling.profile_period was changed (PGC_SIGHUP, no restart, nothing
else different), 2 repeats per cell, same window and workload as above.

  run         nominal  effective   pgws bias   pgsa bias   pgws CPU   collector
                rate      rate     pp of wall  pp of wall   share     on-CPU
  --------------------------------------------------------------------------
  satp201      50 Hz     50.0 Hz     -2.21       -4.67      60.20 %    0.33 %
  satp202      50 Hz     50.0 Hz     -2.80       -3.57      59.52 %    0.38 %
  (main, sat) 100 Hz    100.0 Hz     -2.67 mean  -4.27 mean 59.79 %    0.52 %
  satp011    1000 Hz    828.2 Hz     -1.60       -3.83      59.73 %    2.61 %
  satp012    1000 Hz    820.1 Hz     -1.58       -3.75      59.97 %    2.73 %
  unsatp201    50 Hz     50.0 Hz     -1.14       -2.04      66.79 %    0.33 %
  unsatp202    50 Hz     50.0 Hz     -1.04       -1.48      66.65 %    0.35 %
  (main,unsat)100 Hz    100.0 Hz     -2.02 mean  -1.32 mean 66.52 %    0.49 %
  unsatp011  1000 Hz    900.5 Hz     -1.69       -2.43      68.33 %    2.86 %
  unsatp012  1000 Hz    902.4 Hz     -2.00       -0.54      67.48 %    2.85 %

(a) RULED OUT. Across a 20x range of sampling rate the collector's saturated
bias stays between -1.6 and -2.8 pp while the pg_stat_activity poller, at a
rate the collector also ran at, stays between -3.6 and -4.7 pp. At the SAME
50 Hz the collector is biased -2.2/-2.8 and the poller -4.7/-3.6. Rate is not
the difference; the observer's own service demand is, exactly as section 2
says.

(b) RULED OUT as the explanation of the unsaturated residual. If a 10 ms
period aliasing against a ~1.67 ms transaction cycle were responsible, moving
to 1 ms or 20 ms would move it. It does not: unsaturated the collector reads
-1.0, -1.1 (20 ms), -0.8..-3.1 (10 ms), -1.7, -2.0 (1 ms).

WHAT THE UNSATURATED RESIDUAL IS, THEN: open. Unsaturated, BOTH in-database
samplers show a small persistent negative bias — collector -1.0 to -3.1 pp,
poller -0.5 to -2.4 pp — in a regime where the run queue holds only 2.9-3.1 %
of on-or-runnable time and both observers reach a processor in 8-16 us. It
therefore cannot be run-queue displacement, and it is not period aliasing.
Its size is comparable to the run-to-run spread and is a few times the
estimator's own sampling error (about 0.5 pp at 2 backends), so this
experiment does not resolve it. It is recorded as open rather than explained,
and it is worth noting that #115's reconciliation measured -0.69 pp for the
poller unsaturated where this run measures -1.32 pp: same order, different
machine-hour.

A SECOND, SEPARATE LOAD-CORRELATED EFFECT, found by these controls: at
profile_period = 1 ms the collector cannot keep its own schedule, and it
misses MORE when the machine is busier — 828/820 Hz saturated against
900/902 Hz unsaturated, both against a nominal 1000 Hz. That is collector.c's
"we deliberately skip some samplings ... if processor is working overtime"
path firing, confirmed independently by the tracepoints (834 wakeups/s
saturated, 901/s unsaturated) and by the profile's own row counts. At the
default 10 ms and at 20 ms it holds exactly 100.00 and 50.0 Hz in every run,
so the default is safe at this scale — but the delivered rate is not
guaranteed under load, and the samples it drops are by construction the ones
from the busiest moments.

Overhead also scales as expected with rate: 0.33 % of one processor at 50 Hz,
0.52 % at 100 Hz, 2.7 % at 1000 Hz (8 backends).

================================================================================
6. VERDICT
================================================================================

1. CONFIRMED, mechanism and all. pg_wait_sampling's collector is displaced by
   the scheduler in exactly the way our own sampled tier is: saturated, the
   backends are on-CPU-or-runnable 73.00 % of wall but only 70.33 % of wall
   at the instants the collector actually probed. -2.67 pp of wall, which is
   -2.96 pp of the CPU share it reports. The cause is visible in its own
   scheduling: 16-32 us to reach a processor unsaturated, 256-512 us at the
   90th percentile saturated, and 0.65 s spent waiting for a processor
   against 0.21 s spent running.
2. ITS BIAS IS ABOUT HALF OURS, and the reason is measurable, not
   architectural luck: a probe costs it 42 us of CPU against roughly twice
   that for a pg_stat_activity poller, so it waits half as long for a
   processor and its instants are displaced half as far. Rate is ruled out as
   the explanation. The advantage should shrink as the ProcArray walk gets
   longer with backend count, which 8 backends cannot show.
3. IT IS FAIRER THAN A pg_stat_activity POLLER IN TWO MORE WAYS: it cannot
   produce a torn row (one field, one read), and it holds its nominal rate
   exactly at 10-20 ms periods where a sleep-based poller drifts.
4. BUT ITS BIGGEST ERROR IS NOT SCHEDULING. Having no `state` column, it
   counts every not-waiting backend as CPU, including out-of-command protocol
   work. That makes its number a whole-backend one; read as an in-command CPU
   share it would be ~20 pp too high saturated. Compared correctly, it lands
   59.79 % against our exact tier's 62.27 % and the kernel's 67.99 %.
5. FOR US: nothing here changes #115's conclusions. It adds a second,
   independent in-database sampler that carries the same bias in the same
   direction, which strengthens the finding that
   tests/test_cross_validate_tiered.sh grading exact against sampled under
   saturation measures scheduler bias rather than tracer accuracy.
